Second Wind

A box fan, one really good filter, and a little monitor that keeps score. Second Wind is a volunteer project to build the freshest air we can for the dogs in CACC's pavilions.

Nothing has been built and nothing has been placed at the shelter. This page is the proposal, written down so you can read it and decide whether you want in.

Why air matters

Dogs in any shelter spend most of the day breathing indoor air they share with a lot of neighbors. That is why ventilation and filtration turn up in every set of shelter care standards, and why the Association of Shelter Veterinarians' Guidelines for Standards of Care in Animal Shelters ask for roughly 10 to 20 fresh air changes an hour in animal housing. Good air is not a nice extra in that world. It is part of the standard.

Shared air

Group housing means one room's air belongs to everybody in it. Whatever is floating in it gets shared too: dust, dander, and the fine particles that respiratory aerosols travel on. Pushing that air through a good filter pulls particles out of circulation instead of leaving them to settle and get stirred up again.

Dander and dust

Dogs shed. Fur, dander, and the fine dust that comes with bedding, food, and daily cleaning stay airborne for a long time before they land. Cleaning handles what settles on surfaces. Filtration is the part that works on what is still in the air.

Smoke days

Recent Chicago summers have brought stretches where wildfire smoke drifting in from far away pushes outdoor fine particle levels up for days at a time. On those days opening a window is not the answer, and indoor filtration is the tool that is left. The EPA studied that exact problem, and the design below is what came out of it.

The build

It is deliberately boring. A 20 inch box fan. A 20 by 20 by 4 inch MERV 13 filter held against the intake side. A cheap 20 by 20 by 1 inch fiberglass prefilter in front of that, to catch fur and the big dust before it reaches the good filter. Two silicone straps hold the sandwich together, so there is no tape and swapping a filter takes seconds.

This is the EPA tested do-it-yourself air cleaner in its single filter form, a simpler sibling of the Corsi-Rosenthal box that a lot of people built in 2020. One small detail carries more weight than it looks: the airflow arrow printed on the filter frame has to point at the fan, so the fan pulls air through the filter rather than pushing against it.

We will build these the way the EPA describes on its DIY air cleaner research page, and that page is the instruction manual we follow. This page is the why. The EPA page is the how.

MERV 13, explained

MERV is a filter's report card. The number says how much of the small stuff a filter catches as air passes through it. Higher is not automatically better: a filter that catches more also pushes back harder on the fan, so the trick is picking the highest rating a box fan can still move a useful amount of air through. That is MERV 13.

Ultrafine0.3 to 1 micron: wildfire smoke
~50%
Fine1 to 3 microns: respiratory aerosols
~85%
Coarse3 to 10 microns: dust, pollen, kennel fluff
~90%

These are the minimum capture rates a filter has to hit in each size band to earn a MERV 13 rating, as defined in ASHRAE Standard 52.2. They are per pass: one trip through the filter. A box fan cycles a room's air past that filter many times an hour, which is where the real work happens.

That middle band is the one worth pausing on. Respiratory aerosols travel on particles in these size ranges, and shelter medicine guidance points at the same filter tier: the Koret Shelter Medicine Program at UC Davis writes that a filter rated MERV 13 or better can capture most of the particles that carry pathogens in the air.

Why not just buy HEPA purifiers?

Per pass, HEPA wins and it is not close: a true HEPA filter catches around 99.97% of particles at the hardest size to catch. The catch is what it costs to get air through it. HEPA media is dense, so the machine needs a stronger motor, and that combination is why a commercial unit sized for a big room costs several hundred dollars and takes proprietary filters at proprietary prices.

In a large open room, the number that matters is not per-pass efficiency but how much clean air you get per hour, and per dollar. A box fan moving a lot of air through a decent filter, several times over, can come out ahead of one expensive machine moving less air through a perfect one.

None of which makes HEPA wrong. For a small enclosed room, or somewhere a machine has to be quiet and out of the way, a HEPA unit is the better answer. For big open rooms on a volunteer budget, a fan and a MERV 13 filter is hard to beat.

What we are proposing

Everything on this page is a proposal. It happens only in coordination with shelter staff, and only as CACC approves it. Nothing has been built, nothing has been placed, and the shelter decides what, if anything, ever happens.

  1. Trial

    We are proposing to start small: one pavilion, two units, and one monitor. Run it for a few weeks with the shelter's approval, watch what the monitor says before and after, and find out what actually happens to a box fan in a busy room. If it is a nuisance, we learn that here rather than seven rooms later.

  2. Roll out

    If the trial goes well and the shelter wants more, the goal is two units for each dog pavilion, built and maintained by volunteers as CACC allows. Full coverage would look like this:

  3. Keep score

    The monitors are the honest part. They may rotate between pavilions as the shelter allows, so each room gets a real before and after instead of a guess. If the shelter is comfortable with it, we may chart the numbers here someday.

What the monitor tells us

PM2.5
Fine particles: the smoke and dust that stays in the air longest. This is the number the filters are aimed at, and the one that should move when a unit is running.
CO2
Really a stand-in for fresh air. When a room's carbon dioxide climbs, its air is being rebreathed rather than replaced. The Koret Shelter Medicine Program at UC Davis suggests keeping it at or below 800 parts per million. A filter does not change this number at all, which is exactly why it is useful: it tells us when the answer is ventilation, not filtration.
TVOC and NOx
Rough indicators for gases and odors: cleaning products, off-gassing, traffic fumes drifting in. Not precision instruments, but good at showing that something changed.
Temperature and humidity
Comfort, and physics. Very dry and very damp air behave differently, and somewhere in the 40 to 60 percent relative humidity band is the usual guidance for indoor spaces.
  • Green: everyday
  • Yellow: worth a look
  • Orange: climbing
  • Red: something is going on

The monitor carries a strip of light on its face that changes color with the current particle reading, so anyone walking past can read the room without opening anything. The thresholds behind those colors live in the device's firmware and can be changed, so treat the light as a glance rather than a measurement.

If you have not seen one: it is a small box that mounts on a wall, with a little screen and that light strip along the front. The monitors report over their own connection.

We use AirGradient ONE monitors, bought at retail. We have no connection to the company; it is just the monitor we picked.

If you build or run a fan and filter unit anywhere, follow the fan manufacturer's instructions and the EPA's published guidance, and use a UL or ETL certified box fan made in 2012 or later. Questions

What about fan safety?

In testing by the EPA and UL's Chemical Insights Research Institute, five models of box fan were run with clean filters, with smoke loaded and dust loaded filters, and in an extreme scenario where the front and back of the fan were both blocked for seven hours. Throughout that testing, component temperatures stayed below recognized temperature safety standards, and none of the scenarios tested posed any observable fire hazards.

The EPA is careful about what that does and does not mean. It notes it cannot assure those results are representative of every do-it-yourself air cleaner in every scenario, because builds and materials vary so widely, and that operating any electrical device carries inherent risk.

So we follow its precautions rather than our own judgment: UL or ETL certified box fans made in 2012 or later, which have fused plugs and thermal cutoffs; nothing blocking either side of the fan; and filters changed on a schedule instead of run until they clog.

What about cords, around dogs?

Cords are the part we take most seriously. A unit would go where staff want it, with the cord routed and secured out of dogs' reach and clear of walkways, and nothing set up where it could be knocked over.

Placement would be the shelter's call in every case. If staff wanted a unit moved, turned off, or taken out entirely, it would be moved, turned off, or taken out, without a conversation about it.

Will they be noisy?

A box fan on a low setting is steady white noise, nearer a bathroom fan than a hair dryer. It does not start, stop, chime, or cycle, and steady sound is generally less startling than sudden sound.

That said, a pavilion is a loud room already, and how a fan lands in one is exactly the kind of thing we would rather learn from a trial than assume. If a unit turns out to be a problem in a particular room, it comes out of that room.

How often do the filters get changed?

The cheap fiberglass prefilter is the sacrificial layer. It catches fur and the big dust for a dollar or two and gets swapped often. The 4 inch MERV 13 behind it stays cleaner because of that, and should last months rather than weeks.

The plan is to check monthly and replace when a filter looks visibly dirty. A gray filter is a filter that has been working. This is not optional maintenance, either: the EPA's testing found that do-it-yourself air cleaners were almost completely ineffective with dirty filters.

The silicone straps exist for this reason. Every swap is tool free and takes seconds, which is the difference between maintenance that happens and maintenance that does not.

What do the lights on the monitor mean?

The light strip tracks the current fine particle reading. Green is the everyday state and the warmer colors mean the number is climbing. Because the thresholds are set in firmware and can be adjusted, the colors are a glance and not a measurement.

There is more about each reading, and what it would and would not tell us, in What we are proposing.

Why not HEPA purifiers instead?

Short version: HEPA catches more per pass, but costs far more per unit of air moved, and in a big open room the total air cleaned per hour matters more than the percentage caught on any single pass. HEPA is still the better answer for a small enclosed space.

The longer version, with the tradeoffs laid out, is in MERV 13, explained.

Will this stop dogs from getting sick?

No single thing does. Respiratory bugs (what shelter folks call kennel cough) spread more easily anywhere many dogs share air; that is true of every shelter on earth. They also spread through contact, not just air, which is why the layers that do the heavy lifting are ventilation, cleaning protocols, vaccination, and housing practices, the shelter's programs, run by its staff.

What filtration adds is fewer airborne particles for germs to ride on, and we will let the monitor numbers speak for themselves. The EPA says much the same about its own design: portable air cleaners are one part of an indoor air quality strategy, and do not replace the need for ventilation.

How you can help

Second Wind is an informal project run by CACC volunteers. It is not a charity, a nonprofit, or a program of CACC, the City of Chicago, or any other organization. Anything you chip in is a personal gift toward parts and is not tax deductible.

Sponsor the parts

The best way to sponsor is to buy the parts yourself and bring them to a build day. Ask in the volunteer channels and we will tell you exactly what to get. If buying and hauling is not your thing, a few of us split costs informally; ask in the channel and we will sort it out.

What that covers is short and specific: box fans, MERV 13 filters, fiberglass prefilters, silicone straps, and monitors. Nothing else.

Sponsor a fan, cover a round of filters, chip in on a monitor. Whatever size fits.

Lend your hands

When builds happen we will need hands, and none of it is skilled work:

  • Build days. Strapping filters to fans. About five minutes a unit once you have done one.
  • Filter swaps. Pulling a dirty prefilter and sliding a clean one in, during a shift you are already working.
  • Monitor glances. Reading a number off a small screen and passing it along.

Right now we are gauging interest rather than building a schedule, so simply knowing you would be up for it is genuinely useful.

Either way: say so in the volunteer channels you already use. Whoever shared this link with you can point you at the right thread.
